Why "old methods" are suboptimal underwater:
Adaptive filtering: LMS / NLMS / RLS
The most common method for suppressing direct waves in radar is adaptive cancellation: using the direct wave as a reference signal, and using LMS/NLMS/RLS to estimate the distortion at the receiver end, then subtract it from the received signal. This approach has a hard drawback in underwater applications - it's iterative and not robust against large SNR differences. Simulations (5000 Monte Carlo runs) show that when the SNR gap between the echo and the direct wave reaches ISUDC system typical levels of about 30 dB, LMS and NLMS quickly converge towards the "no suppression" baseline, essentially failing; RLS is slightly better, but it also fails to sustain a gap of over 40 dB. Underwater continuous wave data volume is large, and the computational burden of adaptive point-by-point updates is also a real issue.

The idea is sound, but when ported to underwater applications, there are two pitfalls:
Pitfall one: Strong multipath is missed. Multipath is a time delay copy of the transmitted signal, with similarly high correlation peaks. Traditional CLEAN only performs "one global maximum judgment" - clearing the strongest one at each round, then waiting for the next round to clear another. However, if the residual peak-to-average ratio has already fallen below the threshold after a certain round, the algorithm stops, leaving those "secondary" multipaths untouched, and echoes are still obscured.
Pitfall two: Continuous wave instantaneous frequency changes. Integrated waveforms are continuous waves that significantly change over time; further compounded by absorption variations in the hydroacoustic channel, the amplitude of the direct wave is actually a function of time (frequency). Traditional CLEAN assumes that the amplitude and delay remain constant over the integration time, leading to direct reconstruction - resulting in distorted direct waves compared to the real ones, making reduction ineffective.
Three. DW-CLEAN: Two layers of windows, each handling its own task. The improvement in this paper is summarized as follows: transforming "global cleaning" into "window-by-window cleaning" and "full-length reconstruction" into "segmented reconstruction followed by stitching." This is known as double-layer window CLEAN (DW-CLEAN).
1. Window 1: Time alignment + cleanup of multipaths
The length of the emission signal is used as the window length, sliding along the received signal with a step size. Within each window position, the cross-correlation between transmission and reception is performed, the peak-to-average ratio within the window is recorded, and the peak position is noted. If the peak-to-average ratio in any window exceeds a threshold, it is determined that there is a strong component (direct wave or strong multipath) to be suppressed and located and cleaned accordingly.

2. Window 2: Segmental reconstruction, combatting time-varying frequency alignment
After the direct wave energy and correlation are high and prominent at the front of the window due to the time-varying frequency alignment, the second layer segmentally cuts the uniformly sliced sections (numbered), ensuring that each section has "enough time" for its amplitude and delay to be approximately constant—the correlation between instantaneous frequency and time is cut off at this layer.

Each segment undergoes the standard CLEAN estimation-reconstruction-subtraction process:
Iteratively estimate, reconstruct, and subtract residual peaks until the residual peak-to-average ratio falls below a certain threshold, then save the result of each segment. After processing all segments, they are concatenated back into a single signal output for direct wave suppression:
The length of Window 2 is rigidly constrained by the paper: each segment must be less than half the length of Window 1. The reason is straightforward: if the segment windows overlap, the direct wave will be doubled in the overlapping part; if the segment extends beyond the range of Window 1, the signal lengths on both ends will not align, making subtraction impossible.
Regarding the number of segments, the paper conducts convergence analysis: the more segments there are, the shorter the integral time for each segment, and the better the suppression of the time-varying instantaneous frequency; however, the peak-to-average ratio per segment will increase, and the detection probability will decrease accordingly—therefore, a reasonable range exists where it is impossible to continuously refine. In the stability simulation, thresholds dB and segment numbers were chosen, and these parameters were used for comprehensive comparison with LMS/NLMS/RLS/CLEAN.
